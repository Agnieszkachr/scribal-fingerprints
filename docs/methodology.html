Methodology

The pipeline turns the TEI XML transcriptions of five manuscripts into annotated variant tables, embeds each variant in its context, and tests the resulting manuscript-level profiles with resampling procedures that respect the dependence between neighbouring variants. The normaliser, the function-word list and the language model are its only language-specific components.

Transcriptions and Baseline

The five witnesses are taken from the ITSEE digital transcriptions (Institute for Textual Scholarship and Electronic Editing, University of Birmingham). Each transcription is parsed into a sequence of tokens per verse:

  • only the first hand is read; corrector readings are ignored;
  • a word carried across a line, column or page break is one token;
  • nomina sacra are expanded from a table of the abbreviations attested in the five transcriptions, and the supralinear stroke for a final ν is resolved;
  • marginal sigla, running titles, editorial notes and punctuation are not tokens; tokens are lower-cased and stripped of accents, breathings, diaeresis and iota subscript;
  • every divergence that contains or adjoins a physical gap in the manuscript is excluded;
  • editorially supplied text counts as attested; the robustness configuration drops the 115 variants (2.6 %) that rest on it.

Codex Vaticanus (B) is the baseline: near-complete for John and long regarded as restrained in its interventions. It is a methodologically consistent point of departure, not an assertion of textual superiority. Every analysis is repeated with Codex Alexandrinus (A) and Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ) as baselines, and the typology also with the SBL edition. A critical text is not the primary anchor because it is not a witness: measured against it, a profile would combine what a manuscript did with what the editors decided.

Variant Annotation

Each witness verse is aligned with the baseline verse by longest-common-subsequence matching on normalised tokens. Normalisation folds the alternations that are orthographic rather than textual (itacism, ω/ο, movable ν and ς, elision, consonant gemination), so that they neither break the alignment nor surface as variants. Every non-equal block of the alignment becomes one variant, annotated by a deterministic, inspectable rule; no model is trained, which is why the procedure is called annotation rather than classification.

TypeDefinitionExample
Addition The baseline lacks the word(s); the witness has them — → δέ
Omission The baseline has the word(s); the witness lacks them αὐτοῦ → —
Word-order shift The same words in a different order ηγαπησα υμας → υμας ηγαπησα
Morphological substitution Different words that are forms of the same lexeme (suppletive forms included) λέγει → λέγουσιν
Lexical substitution Different words that are not forms of the same lexeme κύριος → θεός

Transpositions and unequal blocks

Longest-common-subsequence alignment never emits a transposition as one block: A B → B A arrives as an omission and an addition, and A x B → B x A as two substitutions. Word-order shifts are therefore recovered after alignment: an omission and an addition (or two substitutions) whose words are the same multiset, at most eight baseline tokens apart within a verse, are merged into one word-order variant. A replacement block is morphological only if both sides have the same number of words and every positionally paired word is a form of the same lexeme; a block of unequal length is one lexical substitution and is not decomposed, because the alignment cannot say which word was replaced and which added.

Same lexeme or different lexeme

The decision uses a form-to-lemma lexicon of every word form of the printed New Testament (MorphGNT on the SBL edition; 17,347 accentless forms). Suppletive forms (λέγω/εἶπον) fall together and compounds (ἐρωτάω/ἐπερωτάω) fall apart. Crasis is resolved first. A witness form absent from the lexicon is looked up again on its normalised key; if it is still not found, it counts as a form of its partner’s lexeme when its character similarity to some attested form of that lexeme is at least 0.8. Only pairs in which neither word can be placed fall back on the similarity of the two words to each other (same lexeme above 0.5).

Checks against a human reading

Calibration. 200 substitution pairs drawn with a fixed seed, 25 from each of eight bands of character similarity, were annotated blind. The lexicon rule agrees in 94.0 % (κ = 0.87); character similarity alone agrees in 78.0 % at a threshold of 0.5 and at most 81.0 % at any threshold. The handling of crasis and witness spellings was added after the errors of the plain lexicon lookup (87.5 %) had been read, so these figures are in-sample.

Reliability. A stratified sample of 300 variants was annotated blind into the five types. Agreement with the rule is κ = 0.97 overall; it is complete for additions, omissions and word-order shifts, and κ = 0.88 on the morphological/lexical distinction, where all eight disagreements lie (mostly corrupted witness spellings). The samples, the annotations and the evaluation script are in annotation/.

Contextual Embedding

Ancient-Greek-BERT is a transformer language model trained on classical and literary Greek. It is used only as a fixed function from a short Greek phrase to a vector of 768 numbers. Each variant is represented by a window of up to five words on either side of the divergence, in the baseline and in the witness; each window’s vector is the attention-masked mean of the last-layer token vectors. The change vector of a variant is embedding(witness window) − embedding(baseline window). A similarity score (as in BERTScore) would say how far a witness departs; the difference keeps the direction of the departure, so that the variants of one witness can be averaged into a profile. All distances are cosine distances.

Feature isolation

  • Function words only: the 1,767 variants whose readings consist entirely of closed-class words; the contexts are untouched.
  • Content masked: every variant, with each content word in the readings and the context windows replaced by a placeholder.
  • Random masked (control): every variant, with the same number of words per field replaced at random positions, in five independent draws.

Statistical Inference

Several variants can occur in one verse and their context windows overlap, so the change vectors are not independent observations. Inference uses the chapter as the resampling block: block bootstrap (chapters resampled with replacement, 2,000 replicates) for confidence intervals, and block permutation (the witness labels of individual variants shuffled within chapters; 2,000 permutations for Cohen’s d, 200 for Cramér’s V and the ARI, 500 for the split-half geometry) for the null distributions, with the +1 correction of Phipson and Smyth (2010). As a second guard, the change vectors of one witness in one verse are averaged into a single vector before d is recomputed, and this verse-level d is tested against its own null, in which whole witness–verse units are shuffled within chapters.

  • Cramér’s V: association between witness and variant type, on three broad classes and on the substitution sub-classes.
  • Cohen’s d: standardised difference between the mean inter-manuscript and the mean intra-manuscript cosine distance, for the whole corpus, for each pair of witnesses and within each variant class.
  • Adjusted Rand Index: agreement between a k-means partition (k = 4, ten initialisations, 3,000 vectors) and the true witness labels; 0 is chance, 1 perfect recovery.
  • Split halves: profiles computed on odd and on even chapters; the full 8 × 8 distance matrix between half-profiles, the same-witness against the cross-witness distance, the number of witnesses nearest to their own other half, and the rank correlation of the six inter-witness distances, on the full text and on the 532 verses attested in all five witnesses.
